Summary

Goal. Fine-tune π₀.₅ with a RECAP-style advantage tag ("this action was better / worse than usual", from a learned value function) and beat the base policy on one LIBERO task.
  • A fine-tune recipe that keeps base performance. Fine-tuning on our rollouts used to cost ~13–16 points. Making demos 75% of the training frames and using a 10× lower learning rate scored 53.6% vs base 52.4% (one seed; step 2000). Policy chart →
  • The advantage tag is still (almost) unused, and the reason is the labels, not the model. A planted tag of RECAP's expected size is learned on every route, so the conditioning works. Our labels only support a tiny tag-dependent action difference. Changing the value function does not help, and collecting more rollouts from the same policy does not help either: the policy's own data has little better-vs-worse action contrast. Tag-effect chart → Label-signal chart →
  • Best result so far: advantage weighting on the successful rollouts, 56.7% (+4.2 points; 95% CI −3.0 to +11.5, so not yet different from base). Policy chart →

Advantage Guidance (Zhou & Levine): contrastive guidance and the residual parameterisation

running 2026-11-10 – 11-11

At sampling time, each denoising step uses v = v_ref + w·(v₊ − v₋): the + and − tag velocities of the same model, with the no-tag branch as v_ref. w scales a direction the model already learned; nothing is retrained. The residual model keeps π₀.₅ frozen as v_ref and adds a small, zero-initialised tag-conditioned head; residual and AdaRMS are compared at the same action shift, not the same w (w means different amounts in each). Change in success vs base on the same 330 trials.

  • w 8 = 65.8%, +13.3 points [+7.0, +19.7]; 8 of 10 start states improve. Still above zero when resampling whole start states (+0.6 to +25.8).
  • Dose-response: w 2 and w 4 are below base, w 8 above it, and pointing the guidance the wrong way (w −4) costs 29 points. The checkpoint's own reference (w 0) is 13.9 points below base, so w 8 is worth ~27 points relative to it.
  • Why w 8 and not the paper's 1–4: the paper's theorem makes w an inverse temperature (1/β = w·[T(y₊) − T(y₋)]). Our labels are weak (corr 0.10 with the true advantage), which flattens P(+ | A), so a larger w is needed for the same tilt [our reading].
  • Open: w was chosen on these trials; one training seed; the shuffled-label control decides whether the direction is advantage or demo-vs-rollout.

The Nov 10 update

The summary at the top of the page and everything below this line are the update as sent for the Nov 10 meeting.

How I got here

  1. Starting point (week 8 page). Fine-tuning hurt (base 56.4% → ~41%, older eval settings), and the tagged policy ignored its tag, even when the tag was the true outcome. Two problems: the fine-tune costs performance, and the tag is not learned. Week 8 →
  2. Fine-tune cost. Successes only + frozen vision encoder, or a 10× lower learning rate, brought success back to ~52–55%. The training seed alone moves success by ~10 points (same recipe: 63.9% vs 51.8%), so every recipe now needs 2 seeds. Policy chart →
  3. Closing the gaps to RECAP (list below). After each fix, flipping the tag still moved the action by only ~1.3–1.9% of the policy's own sampling noise. RECAP gaps →
  4. Can the model learn a tag at all? Yes. A planted tag that shifts the action by RECAP's expected amount is learned on the prompt route and on the AdaRMS route (0.36× noise). Tag-effect chart →
  5. Are our labels informative? Barely. An offline test (the "coach test") fits the best possible tag-conditioned action model to our data: our labels support an action difference of 0.015 vs 0.004 for random labels and 0.05 for a positive control. Mostly gripper timing. Label-signal chart →
  6. Is the value function the problem? No. A TD critic, a ResNet + π₀.₅ ensemble, longer lookaheads, hindsight returns and the episode outcome all give weaker labels than the current ResNet. Ground truth from the simulator (restore the state, run 12 continuations) shows the true advantage is itself mostly noise; our labels agree with it only weakly (r = 0.11). Label-signal chart → Branching chart →
  7. Is it the amount of data? No. As episodes grow from 112 to 900, the labels' action information levels off by ~450 episodes, while a real signal (the positive control) keeps rising. A round of rollouts with 2× exploration noise adds only a little. Data-curve chart →
  8. Where the fine-tune cost comes from. Training untagged on all rollouts (successes + failures), as RECAP does, drops success to 39.1%: the failure data causes the earlier drop. Tags or advantage weights recover part of it (40.9–52.4%), but not past base. Raising the demo share and lowering the learning rate is what brought it back to base. Policy chart →

Discrepancies found while checking the results

  • The AdaRMS tag is not fully ignored. With real labels, flipping the AdaRMS tag moves the action by 0.0060 (13.6% of noise), about 40% of what the labels can support and ~8× the prompt-route runs (0.0008); its training loss is also ~1% lower with the correct tag. Earlier summaries said "tag ignored on every route". That is true for the prompt routes, not for AdaRMS. Its eval with the + tag was 40.9%; it was never evaluated with the − tag, so we do not know if this learned shift changes success.
  • The data-curve rule I set used the wrong metric. I first read "flat" from the ideal shift, but the ideal shift stays flat with more data even for the positive control (it measures effect size, not learnability). The right read is action information: the labels level off by ~450 episodes while the positive control keeps rising. Same conclusion, better reason.
  • Eval noise is ±5 points. Base π₀.₅ scored 56.4% under the older eval settings and 52.4% under the current ones (matched noise); one success-only checkpoint scored 52.1% and 55.5%. One run (demos 75%, low lr) scored 53.6% at step 2000 and 47.0% at step 5000.
  • The prompt-route models react to whether the tag text is there, not to its value. Removing the tag moves the action ~6× more than flipping + to − (0.0045 vs 0.0008).
  • Branching: the sim restore was not exact at 25% of branch points (state difference > 1e-3). The results are unchanged when those points are dropped (true-advantage reliability 0.33, label correlation 0.14).

How much could the action depend on the tag, given the labels? (coach test)

settled 2026-11-06 – 11-09

Fit the best tag-conditioned action model f(state, tag) on the rollouts and measure how much its action changes between + and −. This is an upper bound on what a policy could learn from those labels. Random labels give the floor.

  • Current labels: 0.015, vs 0.004 for random. The trained prompt-route policies learned 0.0008 of it (5%), AdaRMS 0.0060 (40%).
  • No other value function does better: TD critic 0.013, ensemble 0.012, outcome 0.011, longer lookaheads 0.009–0.010, hindsight 0.009.

Ground truth from the simulator: when is a failure decided?

settled 2026-11-09

For 12 failed rollouts, restore the exact sim state every 20 steps and run 12 fresh continuations of the base policy. V* = share that succeed (the true chance of success at that moment). The strip under each plot shows the label our pipeline gave that moment (+ or −).

  • The true chance of success is measured reliably: V* split-half 0.90. In these examples a failure is decided at a few moments: V* falls from ~0.6–0.8 to ~0 within 1–4 branch points (20–80 steps).
  • The true advantage is mostly noise: A* split-half reliability 0.33 (0.50 for all 12 continuations).
  • Our labels agree with it only weakly: correlation with A* 0.11; 37% of moments with A* > 0 are labelled +, vs 23% with A* < 0.
  • So branching-based corrections would need many more continuations, and only at the decision moments.
