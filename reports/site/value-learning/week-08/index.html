Summary

Started from the Nov 3 mentor meeting: get the first policy results, test a second way of making advantage labels, and find which language-model layers carry progress without the flicker.
From the Nov 3 meeting
  • Where we are (week 7): the ResNet value function is the steadier progress tracker; π₀.₅ is more accurate, mainly by recognising hard vs easy scenes from the first frames, but flickers more. Week 7 trade-off →
  • The flicker has two separate sources: π₀.₅'s frozen language-model layers, and fine-tuning its vision encoder. A vision-encoder-only value function never beat the ResNet (frozen: steady but less accurate; fine-tuned: accurate but noisy). Week 7 vision-encoder test →
  • SeeQ finds a VLM without pre-training performs on par with a ResNet for value learning. Given compute, hold off on pre-training for now and keep pushing with smaller models and limited data.
  • Advantage labels: training V on all 900 rollouts and labelling the same rollouts could be circular. Three valid options: (1) train on all, label all (simplest; risk of degenerate advantages if V memorises outcomes), (2) a new held-out rollout set (cleanest; 2× the data), (3) 5-fold out-of-fold labels (most principled; already running). Run (1) too, for comparison.
  • Subtask: a quick test reads which pot the robot is on with ~97% accuracy, so the signal is there. SeeQ's full method (a subtask-scoped Q-function, different policy extraction) is still worth trying.
  • New direction from SmolVLA / SmolVLM: SmolVLA throws away the top half of its language model and loses little on LIBERO. Apply the same idea to the value function: find which π₀.₅ layers it needs.

Scene calibration: do the value functions know which scenes are hard?

settled 2026-11-03

Week 7 found π₀.₅ is more accurate mainly because it recognises hard vs easy scenes from the first frames. Here each dot is one of the 10 validation scenes (12 rollouts each): its actual success rate against the value predicted in the first 2 s of its rollouts.

  • All three rank the scenes about equally well: correlation with the scene's success rate is 0.66 (ResNet), 0.68 (frozen π₀.₅), 0.65 (LoRA). Seed-to-seed spread is about ±0.06, so these are the same.
  • The difference is how far they commit. π₀.₅'s early values spread about twice as wide across scenes (slope against the true target 0.50–0.57 vs 0.23 for the ResNet). The ResNet hedges every scene toward the middle (−0.83 to −0.59, where the target runs −0.93 to −0.39).
  • That is where π₀.₅'s accuracy edge comes from: per-scene error 0.116–0.120 vs 0.150 for the ResNet. So the week 7 claim needs a correction: π₀.₅ is not better at telling hard scenes from easy ones; it is more willing to say so.
  • Why it matters for advantages: an advantage is a change in V within an episode, so a scene-wide offset cancels out. The ResNet's under-commitment at the start costs accuracy but not advantage quality, consistent with its advantage labels doing better within scenes.
  • Caveats: 10 scenes only, so each correlation is uncertain (95% range for r = 0.66 is roughly 0.05 to 0.91); the spread and per-scene error differences are the firmer result. LoRA is one seed. "First 2 s" = the first 40 environment steps at LIBERO's 20 Hz (see the episode-length card).

Two fine-tune recipes no longer hurt the policy

running 2026-11-05

The original fine-tune drops π₀.₅ from 56.4% to ~41%. Does a frozen vision encoder, a 10× lower learning rate, or stopping earlier keep the base policy's success rate? (Success-only arm, same 330 trials as base.)

  • Frozen vision encoder: 172/330 = 52.1%, −4.2 points vs base (95% CI −10.6 to +2.4).
  • 10× lower learning rate: 172/330 = 52.1%, −4.2 points (95% CI −11.2 to +2.7). The totals tie, but the two recipes win on different scenes.
  • Both meet the bar we set: within ~5 points of base, with the interval covering 0. The original recipe was −15.8 (−22 to −9).
  • Earlier checkpoints (original recipe, evaluated at step 1000 and 3000): running. Does the drop grow with training?
  • Next: a tag run on a recipe that doesn't hurt.

Even a perfect tag is ignored

settled 2026-11-05

Is the tag ignored because the labels are weak, or because the policy can't learn a tag at all? Fine-tune with the true rollout outcome as the tag.

  • Ignored. Flipping the oracle tag moves the action by 2.1% of the policy's own sampling noise. The success-only policy, which never saw a tag, moves 2.6%.
  • The training loss is the same with the correct and the flipped tag (0.00703 vs 0.00703).
  • So weak labels are not the whole story: with this recipe (~52% positive, no tag dropout), even a perfectly informative tag is not learned. Caveat: the oracle tag was one value per episode, so it did not test per-step conditioning.
Source: runs/policy_armC_oracle_s0_18310224, runs/tag_usage_18310225

Fine-tuning makes the policy worse: 56% before, ~41% after

settled 2026-11-04

The fine-tuned policies scored ~41%. How does the original π₀.₅, without any fine-tuning, do on exactly the same 330 trials?

  • Base π₀.₅: 186/330 = 56.4%. Success-only fine-tune 40.6% (−15.8 points, 95% CI −22 to −9); tagged with the positive tag 40.8% (−15.5); negative tag 43.0% (−13.3).
  • The losses are largest on the easiest scenes (91% → 52%, 85% → 61%); two scenes improve (15% → 55%, 61% → 73%), so the fine-tune changes behaviour a lot, mostly for the worse.
  • Together with the tag check: the fine-tune costs ~15 points and the tag was never learned, so the advantage labels have not really been tested yet.
  • Next: find a fine-tune recipe that does not hurt (frozen vision encoder, lower learning rate, earlier checkpoints), then compare labels.

The tagged policy ignores its tag

settled 2026-11-04

The advantage-tagged policy scored the same with the positive and the negative tag. Does it react to the tag at all?

  • Actions: on 320 held-out frames, flipping the tag (same random noise) moves the action by 2% of what the policy's own sampling noise does, and on no frame by more than the noise. That is even less than for the success-only policy, which never saw a tag (4%).
  • Training data: the policy predicts its own training actions exactly as well with the flipped tag as with the correct one (loss 0.00689 vs 0.00689).
  • So the policy never learned what "positive" and "negative" mean; the evaluation result says nothing yet about whether better labels would help.
  • Possible causes: the labels say too little about the actions (73% vs 66% positive in successes vs failures), or a word in the prompt read through a frozen language model is too weak a signal. Next: a fine-tune with an oracle tag (tag = rollout outcome) separates the two.
Source: runs/tag_usage_18309419; reports/2026-11-04-tag-usage/

Truncated π₀.₅ value function: 4 layers is steadier on all 3 seeds

settled 2026-11-05

SmolVLA drops the top half of its language model and loses little. Does a π₀.₅ value function that reads only the first 4 or 9 of the 18 language-model layers flicker less, and is it still as accurate?

  • First 4 layers, 3 seeds: steadiness 0.180 / 0.182 / 0.191 (mean 0.184 ± 0.006), MAE 0.169 / 0.170 / 0.171. All 18 layers: 0.152, MAE 0.160. First 9 (seed 0 only): 0.158, 0.162.
  • So cutting to 4 layers lowers the flicker on every seed (step-to-step noise 0.101 → 0.083), at a cost of ~0.01 MAE. It is the steadiest π₀.₅ value function so far, but still well below the ResNet (0.247).
  • 4 layers + LoRA (seed 0): 0.169, MAE 0.166. That's steadier than LoRA on all 18 layers (0.133, MAE 0.155) but less steady than frozen 4 layers, so LoRA trades the steadiness back for a little accuracy. The pot-on-stove bump is gone.
  • The 4- and 9-layer cuts came from SmolVLA's "keep the first half" before the per-layer probe. The probe puts the flicker in the last 1–2 layers (states 17–18), which points to a cut at 16–17 layers. That cut has not been run. The probe also doesn't explain why 4 layers beats 9: a single-layer head is equally steady at every layer up to 15, so the gain comes from what the value expert reads, not from one steadier layer.

Per-layer probe: the flicker enters in the last 2–3 layers

settled 2026-11-04

Same small value head on frozen features from each of π₀.₅'s 19 language-model layers (825 episodes, 3 seeds): where does steadiness drop, and does accuracy change with depth?

  • Layers 0–15 are about equally steady (0.14–0.16; layer 0 0.156 ± 0.019, layer 13 0.152 ± 0.006). It drops only at the top: layer 17 0.122, layer 18 0.107, final 0.116.
  • The response to the first pot landing on the stove disappears at the same depth (+0.05 at layers 0–15, ~0 at 17+).
  • Accuracy barely changes with depth (MAE 0.173–0.178, 0.184 at layer 18). Separating successes from failures is best in the middle (AUC 0.837 at layer 13 vs 0.797 at layer 0). This is for a small 2-layer head reading one averaged layer at a time. The full π₀.₅ value expert, which attends to every layer's tokens, does gain accuracy from the language model: MAE 0.215 with the vision encoder alone (1 seed), 0.170 with 4 layers, 0.160 with all 18.
  • The earlier probe's "steady fall from layer 1" was small-data noise. Rerun on the same 375 fixed-layout episodes: layer 0 0.107 ± 0.023, layers 1–17 0.079–0.094 (the layer 0 → 1 drop is ~1 sd), final 0.055. On both sets the final layer is worst and the earlier layers are about equal; with more layout variety every layer is steadier and the drop sits clearly at the top.

Before robot training: the top layers were already noisy

settled 2026-11-04

Same per-layer probe on PaliGemma, the vision-language model π₀.₅ started from, before any robot training. Did robot training make the top layers noisy, or were they noisy already?

  • PaliGemma: steadiness drifts down with depth, 0.146 at layer 0 to 0.113 at layers 17–18. π₀.₅: flat around 0.15 through layer 15, then 0.12 / 0.11 at layers 17–18. Both end at about the same level.
  • Robot training made every layer more useful: accuracy MAE 0.195–0.207 → 0.173–0.184 and success-vs-failure AUC 0.66–0.73 → 0.79–0.84; middle layers got steadier (layer 11: 0.119 → 0.159).
  • It also removed something at the top: PaliGemma's top layers still respond when a pot lands on the stove (+0.05); π₀.₅'s do not (≈ 0 from layer 17).
  • So the top-layer noise is inherited, not caused by robot training, and cutting π₀.₅ before layer 17 keeps the layers robot training improved.

First policy results: the advantage tag does nothing

settled 2026-11-04

Does fine-tuning π₀.₅ with ResNet advantage tags (positive = this step made progress) beat fine-tuning on successes only?

  • Success-only: 134/330 = 0.406. Advantage-tagged, positive tag: 135/330 = 0.409. Same policy, negative tag (control): 142/330 = 0.430. The untouched base π₀.₅ scores 0.564 on the same trials (next-but-one card).

  • Setup: 33 rollouts on each of the 10 held-out scenes, environment seed 7, 5000 fine-tuning steps each, ResNet 5-fold out-of-fold labels (20-step lookahead, 70% positive).

π₀.₅ out-of-fold labels: a more accurate value, worse labels

settled 2026-11-04

The ResNet labels gave no policy gain. Would labels from the more accurate π₀.₅ value function (same 5 folds, same rule) be better?

  • Value accuracy: π₀.₅ out-of-fold MAE 0.182 vs ResNet 0.195 (better).
  • Labels: within-scene AUC (does the share of positive steps separate successes from failures in the same scene?) 0.68 vs ResNet 0.74 (worse). Positive share inside successes vs failures: 72% vs 66% (ResNet 73% vs 66%). Near the end of failed rollouts 70% of steps are still positive (ResNet 64%).
  • The two label sets agree on 65% of steps (57% expected by chance); their advantages correlate at only 0.27.
  • Why: an advantage is a change in V over 20 steps, so V's flicker goes straight into the label. π₀.₅'s accuracy comes from judging scene difficulty early, which cancels out in a change; its extra flicker does not.
  • Decision: no policy fine-tune with π₀.₅ labels; both label sets are weak (only ~7 points between successes and failures), which fits the no-gain policy result.
Source: tables/moka_fixed_fresh/labels/oof_expert_n20_p30 vs oof_resnet_n20_p30 (scripts/libero_oof_labels.py)

Steering pre-check: π₀.₅'s candidates are too alike to choose between

settled 2026-11-04

SeeQ-style steering samples several action chunks and executes the one a value function likes best. Before training any Q-function: do π₀.₅'s candidates differ, and does choosing between them change success?

  • Spread: at 920 decision points in 10 rollouts, the 8 sampled chunks end a median 1.9 mm apart, while the arm moves 22.7 mm per chunk. The gripper command differs between candidates at only 7% of decision points.
  • Headroom: at 4 points in each of 10 rollouts, each candidate was run to the end twice from the same exactly-restored simulator state. Picking the best candidate on the first run and scoring it on the second gives +0.006 success (95% CI −0.028 to +0.042), i.e. nothing. The apparent +0.10 for the in-sample best is luck.
  • Decision: stop steering here. If the simulator itself cannot pick a better candidate, a learned Q cannot either. Widening the spread would mean changing the base policy's sampling.
  • Caveat: 10 states and 2 repeats per candidate.
Source: runs/steer_probe_libero_10_18296012; reports/2026-11-04-steer-probe/

Episode length: rollouts are ~20 s of simulated time, not ~40 s

settled 2026-11-03

The mentor noted that ~40 s seemed long for a LIBERO task. How long are the rollouts in environment steps and in seconds?

  • Steps: successful rollouts take 348–516 environment steps (median 404); failures hit LIBERO's 520-step limit for this suite. One recorded frame = one environment step.
  • Seconds: LIBERO's environment runs at 20 Hz (control_freq=20 in LIBERO's env wrapper; our rollout script keeps the default). So a median success is ~20 s of simulated time (348–516 steps = 17–26 s), and the cap is 26 s.
  • Why it looked like ~40 s: the rollout videos are saved at 10 fps (imageio.mimwrite(..., fps=10) in scripts/libero_rollouts.py, copied from openpi's LIBERO example), so they play at half speed. Example: a 382-step success is a 38.2 s video but 19.1 s of robot time.
  • What changes: only time labels. Our scripts assumed 10 Hz, so every "seconds" number on the earlier pages is doubled: the "2 s" steadiness lag and advantage lookahead (20 steps) are really 1 s, "AUC at 5 / 10 s" is at 2.5 / 5 s. The metrics themselves, the labels and the fine-tunes are unchanged, because everything is computed in steps.
  • Week 7 is kept as the pre-meeting snapshot with the old labels. From week 8 on, times are given in steps and real (20 Hz) seconds.
