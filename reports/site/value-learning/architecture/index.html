0 · The loop these models live in

The project is one loop. Each architecture below sits at one point on it.

1 · π₀.₅ as it runs in LIBERO

π₀.₅ is two transformers that run side by side, layer by layer. The backbone (PaliGemma: SigLIP vision encoder + Gemma 2B) reads the images and the task text. The action expert (a smaller Gemma, 300M) holds the 10 action tokens. At every layer the action tokens attend to the backbone's keys and values, so the expert "reads" the scene layer by layer. The expert does not output actions directly: it outputs a velocity that moves noise toward an action chunk, and the model is called 10 times per chunk.

Check: because pi05_libero drops the state from the prompt, the value expert's --blank-state arm (Oct 30, arm D) did not change the input; its "no change beyond seed noise" result is what a no-op would give. Its conclusion (proprioception is not the shortcut) still holds, for a simpler reason: the state never entered.

2 · Who attends to whom

The attention mask explains two things used throughout this project: why the prefix can be cached across the 10 denoising steps, and why a tag placed in the prompt changes the backbone's features while a tag placed in the expert does not.

3 · Flow matching in two lines

The action expert is trained to predict the direction from a noisy chunk back toward the real chunk. At inference it starts from pure noise and follows that direction for 10 steps. Everything later on this page (tags, guidance, the residual head, DSRL) acts on one of the two quantities here: the velocity v or the start noise ε.

train: t ~ Beta(1.5, 1), x_t = t·ε + (1 − t)·a, loss = ‖ v_θ(x_t, t, obs) − (ε − a) ‖² sample: x ← ε ~ N(0, I); repeat 10×: x ← x − 0.1 · v_θ(x, t, obs) → action chunk

Guidance and the residual head change v at every step. DSRL picks ε and leaves v alone.

4 · Fine-tuning π₀.₅ with LoRA, and why it has not helped

Every "train π₀.₅ on our rollouts" run (filtered BC, weighted BC, the prompt and AdaRMS tag routes) uses the same recipe: openpi's LoRA fine-tune, scripts/pi05_policy_finetune.py. LoRA (low-rank adaptation) keeps the pretrained weights fixed and learns a small correction next to each weight matrix. That makes fine-tuning cheap, but it does not stop the policy from changing: the correction is applied on every forward pass, for every state.

One training step

Data and loss
sample: 1 frame of a rollout (2 images, prompt, state) target: the next 10 actions executed from it (on rollouts: halves of two different plans, point 6) loss = flow-matching loss of §3 on that chunk batch = 32 frames (4 micro-batches of 8, gradients summed)

The arm decides which rollouts are in the table: successes only (filtered BC), all rollouts with a tag (A, B), or all rollouts with per-frame weights (weighted BC). Demos or base rollouts can be mixed in as an anchor.

Optimiser and budget
AdamW (b2 0.95), grad clip 1.0 lr: LoRA 1e-4, other weights 2.5e-5 ("low": 10× less) warmup 500 steps, then cosine to 10% 5,000 steps × 32 = 160k frames

160k frames ≈ 0.7 passes over the 516 round-1 successes (216k frames), 0.4 over all 900 rollouts. The val loss (flow loss on val-state frames) is logged as a drift check, not used to pick a checkpoint.

The AdaRMS tag run (§5, panel B) is also a LoRA fine-tune: its untagged branch scores 0.385. The best fine-tune, advantage-weighted BC at step 2000 (0.594, +0.07), is one seed, and two seeds of one recipe differ by up to 0.12 (findings, "Method traps"). No fine-tune is clearly above base; most are clearly below.

Why it has not helped: what the evidence points to

  1. The rollouts hold little to learn beyond what π₀.₅ already does. Every rollout is a sample from π₀.₅ itself (or a policy close to it). Fitting the flow loss on your own samples mostly reproduces your own behaviour. The useful part, how a success differs from a failure from the same state, is small: about 0.015 in raw action, mostly gripper timing (coach test, Nov 8), and the advantage labels track it only weakly (corr 0.04–0.14 with the simulator's true advantage). The val loss shows the same thing: it falls from 0.0104 to 0.0083 in the first 500 steps and then stops improving while the train loss keeps falling.
  2. Nothing holds the policy near base. B = 0 makes step 0 exactly π₀.₅, but after that 52 M parameters move freely at lr 1e-4. With little real signal (point 1), most of what the update does is fit the quirks of ~1,000 episodes of one task, and that applies at every state, including ones the rollouts never covered. Evidence that drift is the problem: two seeds of one recipe land 0.12 apart; mixing in demos pulls the all-rollout run from 0.391 back to 0.515.
  3. The anchor we used is not the base's data. The usual anchor is the data the base was trained on (for a DROID-trained base, DROID itself: large and broad). Ours was 29 moka demos, repeated up to 37× to fill half of each batch, so the model can memorise them, and human teleop moves differently from π₀.₅. With successes-only data there was also nothing bad for the demos to dilute, which is why they helped the all-rollout runs but not round 2 (0.439 vs 0.442 without).
  4. Untagged failures teach failure, and the tag does not separate them. All rollouts with no tag: 0.391. With the advantage tag, the + and − tag give 0.570 and 0.591 at the same checkpoint: the model learned one average policy and ignores the tag at w = 1. Guidance (amplifying the + vs − difference ~9×) is what made the tag matter.
  5. A real gain of ≤ 0.1 is invisible with one seed. Eval noise is ±0.054 per arm at 330 trials and training seeds differ by ~0.1. Small gains or losses from a recipe change (lr, step, anchor share) cannot be told apart from seed luck, which is why the grid above looks like noise around 0.45–0.55.
  6. On rollouts, the training target is not a chunk π₀.₅ ever produced [found Nov 14; effect not yet measured]. The target for a frame is the next 10 actions the robot executed. That is right for demos (one continuous human motion), but a rollout replans every 5 steps: the executed actions are the first 5 of one sampled chunk, then the first 5 of the next. So every 10-step target joins two plans (three for frames between replans), and its second half is not what π₀.₅ had planned for those steps. Measured on base π₀.₅'s own rollouts (9,351 consecutive chunk pairs): the executed steps 5–9 differ from the planned ones by 0.020 per arm dimension (gripper 0.064, with the open/close command flipped in 3% of steps), about the size of the success-vs-failure difference in point 1; at the join the target jumps 0.020 between consecutive steps, 2.4× the normal step-to-step change inside one plan (0.008). Fine-tuning on these targets teaches π₀.₅ a seam that its own sampling never has. Fix: --chunk-targets sampled trains only on replan frames, each with the full 10-action chunk sampled there (saved by the rollout client since Nov 14; demos keep executed targets).
The pattern across the project. Every method that changes π₀.₅'s weights has landed at or below base; every method that keeps π₀.₅ frozen and steers it has gone above (guidance on the residual head: steers the velocity v; DSRL: picks the start noise ε). The base already sits near what imitation of this data can give; the headroom is in choosing among its own behaviours, not in re-fitting them.

Checks that would explain it further

  • Measure drift directly: action difference fine-tuned vs base on val states at steps 500 / 1000 / 2000 / 5000, in units of the sampling noise (the same measure as the tag-shift checks). This shows how far each recipe moves the policy, apart from whether success changes.
  • Train vs val states: success on the 30 training init states vs the 10 val states. If train goes up while val goes down, the update overfits the states it saw.
  • Base-rollout anchor (queued, 18497899): half of each batch from base π₀.₅'s own 900 rollouts instead of demos.
  • Fewer moving parts: LoRA on the action expert only (backbone untouched), or a lower rank, to see whether the backbone's changes cause the drop.
  • Two or three seeds for any recipe whose effect is under 0.1.
  • Chunk targets (queued, Nov 14): the same 300 new base π₀.₅ rollouts fine-tuned twice with filtered BC round 1's recipe, once with executed targets (as every run above) and once with sampled chunks (point 6); only the targets differ. Run alongside the distillation fine-tunes on DSRL and hybrid rollouts (scripts/distill_chain.sh).

5 · Three ways to give π₀.₅ an advantage tag

RECAP-style improvement trains the policy on its own rollouts with a per-frame tag ("this action was better / worse than expected"), then asks for the "better" behaviour at inference. The question was where the tag should enter the network. Same skeleton, three entry points:

How the tag gets into the model in B and C without touching the data pipeline [ours, vl/tag_route.py]: every data tool still writes tagged prompts; the policy's input transform cuts the ". Advantage: …" suffix off before tokenising and carries the tag as two extra boolean entries in the observation's image_masks dict. openpi only reads the camera keys from that dict, so the extra entries pass straight through to compute_loss / sample_actions.

6 · The residual head, in detail

From Advantage Guidance (Zhou & Levine, arXiv 2610.19783, §6.2): split the velocity into a reference that ignores the tag and a residual that sees it,

v(x_t, t, s, y) = stopgrad( v_ref(x_t, t, s) ) + h_θ(x_t, t, s, y)

Because v_ref already explains everything that does not depend on y, the only thing h_θ can learn consistently is the y-dependent part. Our adaptation [ours]: v_ref is base π₀.₅, frozen (it is the policy that collected every training rollout, which is what the paper's theory asks for), and h_θ is a small AdaLN MLP that reads the frozen action expert's last-layer output.

Training vs sampling

Training (ResPi0.compute_loss)
v = v_ref + has_tag · h(y) loss = ‖ v − (ε − a) ‖²

Only res_head gets gradients. Same rollouts + demos and the same binary RECAP labels (vb40) as the AdaRMS run in panel B, so only the parameterisation differs. lr 3e-4 (the paper's actor lr), 5,000 steps, batch 32, tag dropout 0 (the reference is a separate network, so no dropout branch is needed).

Sampling (ResPi0.sample_actions, per Euler step)
feat, v_ref = base(x_t, t) # one π₀.₅ pass v = v_ref + w · ( h(feat, +1) − h(feat, −1) ) x ← x − 0.1 · v

The head runs twice per step but it is tiny; the expensive π₀.₅ pass runs once. With w = 0 the policy is base π₀.₅. w is set from an offline check so the guided action moves 0.99 × the sampling-noise scale (the shift where AdaRMS w 8 sat): w 9.26.

Why it beat the AdaRMS route

  • No base drift. Every fine-tune of π₀.₅ on our rollouts lost ground (the AdaRMS checkpoint's untagged branch: 0.385 vs base 0.524). The residual leaves π₀.₅ untouched, so guidance adds to 0.524 instead of to 0.385.
  • The head can only learn the tag. With one action per state in the data, a conditioned network can lower its loss by ignoring y. Here the tag-independent part is already explained by v_ref, so what is left for h is the + vs − difference.
  • Control: the same AdaRMS recipe with episode-shuffled labels falls to 0.363 at w 8, so the gain comes from the labels, not from guidance alone. (The shuffled control for the residual itself is not run yet.)

7 · Other architectures on the same backbone

Value expert (V on π₀.₅'s own representations)

The same trick as the action expert, used for a critic: a new, narrower transformer reads the frozen backbone's per-layer K/V through one learned query token, and its output feeds a 201-bin value head and a success head. The truncated variant reads only the first N layers (SmolVLA's idea); N = 4 gave the steadiest π₀.₅ critic.

DSRL (online RL in π₀.₅'s noise space)

DSRL never changes π₀.₅'s weights or velocity. A small SAC agent chooses the start noise ε of the flow (§3), and π₀.₅ turns it into an action chunk as usual. The agent learns from task reward, online, which noise vectors lead to success.
